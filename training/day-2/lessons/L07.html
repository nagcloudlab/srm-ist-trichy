Why BCE Loss Fails

The loss function itself is the root cause of GAN instability

Band-Aids vs Surgery

Day 1 · S5 “When GANs Break”

The Uncomfortable Truth

All those tricks fight symptoms.

The disease is the loss function itself — BCE.

Today we understand why. L08 brings the cure.

Saturation — and What Day 1’s Fix Leaves Behind

Problem 1: The Minimax Loss Saturates

Let D(fake) = σ(a), a = D’s logit. Gradient of G’s loss w.r.t. a:

G loss∂L/∂aD(fake) = 0.0001
Minimax: log(1 − D(G(z)))−σ(a)−0.0001 → vanishes
Non-saturating: −log D(G(z))σ(a) − 1−0.9999 → strong
  • Minimax: no signal exactly when G is worst
  • Non-saturating (Goodfellow 2014, used on Day 1): fixes that

Where −0.0001 and −0.9999 come from · worked example

D(G(z)) = σ(a)   ·   minimax: log(1 − σ(a))   ·   non-saturating: −log σ(a)
GIVEN
  • D(G(z)) = 0.0001
  • σ(a) = 1 / (1 + e−a)
  • σ′(a) = σ(a)(1 − σ(a))
  1. 1a = log(0.0001 / 0.9999) = −9.21the fake logit, before the sigmoid
  2. 2∂/∂a log(1 − σ(a)) = −σ(a) = −0.0001minimax: chain rule cancels (1 − σ), leaves tiny σ
  3. 3∂/∂a [−log σ(a)] = σ(a) − 1 = −0.9999non-saturating: cancels tiny σ, leaves 1 − σ
  4. 40.9999 / 0.0001 ≈ 10,000ratio of the two gradient sizes
  5. =non-saturating gives ≈ 10,000× more signal
At D(fake) = 0.5 both gradients are −0.5 — they only differ once D starts winning.

Size survives — direction doesn’t

  • G’s update = gradient flowing through D back to the image
  • A near-perfect D is flat or erratic around the fakes
  • Result: big, noisy, uninformative steps (Arjovsky & Bottou, 2017)

Problem 2: No Overlap → JS Stuck at log 2

Optimal D: V = 2·JS(Pr, Pg) − 2 log 2
No overlap → JS = log 2 ≈ 0.693 → ∇ = 0

No Overlap Is the Normal Case

  • Real digits: a thin low-dimensional manifold in 784-D pixel space
  • Fakes: another thin surface, made from a 64-D z
  • Two thin surfaces almost never overlap
  • ⇒ a near-perfect D is easy to find, and JS is stuck at log 2

Arjovsky & Bottou, 2017

Two piles that never touch: JS = log 2 · worked example

GIVEN
  • Pr: ½ at x = 0, ½ at x = 1
  • Pg: ½ at x = 5, ½ at x = 6
  1. 1M = ¼ at 0, 1, 5, 6each pile contributes half its mass
  2. 2KL(Pr ‖ M) = 2 · ½ · log(½ / ¼) = log 2only Pr’s two points count
  3. 3KL(Pg ‖ M) = log 2same by symmetry
  4. 4JS = ½ log 2 + ½ log 2 = log 2 ≈ 0.693the maximum possible value
  5. 5D*(0) = ½ / (½ + 0) = 1  ·  D*(5) = 0a perfect D exists
  6. 6move Pg to 50, 51 → JS still log 2distance never enters the formula
  7. =BCE value at D* = 2 · log 2 − 2 log 2 = 0, whatever the gap
Same answer for 5 units or 50 units apart → the loss cannot say “closer”.

Training Instability

Problem 3: The Narrow Sweet Spot

The Fundamental Conflict

  • D needs to be good enough to give G useful feedback
  • But if D is too good, the feedback disappears
  • You need D "good but not too good" — hard to control

You spend more time tuning D's strength than actually training the GAN.

Problem 4: Mode Collapse (Again)

What BCE Asks G

BCE: "Can you fool D?"
G: "Yes, this one image works!"
BCE: "Great! Low loss!"

BCE does NOT ask: "Are your outputs varied?"

−log D* trains G on KL(Pg‖Pr) − 2·JS
  • Fake where no real data: huge cost
  • Real mode with no fakes: almost free

What a Better Loss Would Ask

"How similar is your output distribution to the real distribution?"

A missing mode should cost something — mass that still has to be moved.

That's exactly what Wasserstein distance does.

Knowledge Check

Day 1’s G loss is −log D(G(z)). D(fake) = 0.0001. Does G’s gradient vanish?

Click to reveal

No. On D’s logit it is σ(a) − 1 ≈ −0.9999 (minimax would give −0.0001). The real problem: it flows back through a near-perfect D that is flat or erratic around the fakes — big but uninformative steps.

Fakes 1 pixel-shift away from the reals vs 100 pixel-shifts away, supports disjoint. What does JS say? What would you want?

Click to reveal

JS = log 2 ≈ 0.693 for both — it can’t tell “almost there” from “hopeless”. You want a distance that shrinks as the fakes move closer — that is Wasserstein (L08).

Instance noise makes the distributions overlap. If it works, why replace the loss?

Click to reveal

It blurs what D sees, so D judges noisy images, and it must be decayed — which brings the disjoint-support problem back. A loss that is informative without overlap removes the need.
