Recall · Unit 1

A quick warm-up so no one is lost later. Log of 1 is 0. Log of anything between 0 and 1 is negative, and it plunges toward minus infinity as x approaches 0. Recall · Unit 1

Walk through the substitution line by line. y is a switch. With y = 1, the factor (1 − y) is 0, so the second term disappears and only −log p survives: a real image is punished when p is low. With y = 0, the factor y is 0, so the first term disappears and only −log(1 − p) survives: a fake image is punished when p is high. Same numbers on both sides, mirrored: a confident right answer costs 0.11, a confident wrong answer costs 2.30. In one sentence: BCE is minus the log of the probability given to the correct answer.

Part A · §21 · Original WGAN: weight clipping

First fix: clip every weight

for p in critic.parameters():
    p.data.clamp_(-0.01, 0.01)

Tiny weights → the critic can’t change too violently.

Orange weights were outside the range and get pushed onto the walls. Every one ends up at exactly ±0.01: that pile-up is the problem two slides on. After every critic update, force each weight into the range minus 0.01 to plus 0.01. A network with bounded weights is Lipschitz with some constant. Simple to implement, one line of code.

Part A · §22 · Why weight clipping is crude

Clipping works, but it’s a blunt tool

Range too small

Critic too weak; gradients vanish

Range too large

Constraint barely bites; slow, unstable training

Also note

It only guarantees some constant K, so you get W × an unknown scale — and weights pile up at ±0.01, wasting capacity.

The WGAN authors themselves called weight clipping a clearly terrible way to enforce a Lipschitz constraint. The WGAN-GP paper showed that clipped weights collapse onto the two extreme values, and the critic ends up learning overly simple functions. This motivated a better method.

Part A · §22 · Why weight clipping is crude

Clipping squeezes weights to the walls

Weight clipping

spread out, uses its range

A sketch of a finding reported in the WGAN-GP paper, Gulrajani et al. 2017: with weight clipping, most critic weights end up pressed against the two clipping limits, so the network behaves like a much simpler function and models only crude features. Part A · §23 · WGAN-GP

Better fix: speed cameras on the road

real · ◆ checkpoints x̂ between them · fake

fine = λ · (slope − 1)²

At each checkpoint, measure the critic’s slope ‖∇C(x̂)‖.

Slope 1: no fine. Anything else: fined.

Gulrajani et al., 2017. Weight clipping tried to control the critic indirectly through its weights. The gradient penalty measures the thing we actually care about, the critic's slope, but only at a few random checkpoints on the road between a real sample and a fake one, like speed cameras. At each checkpoint x-hat it computes the gradient norm, the slope, and adds a fine of lambda times (slope - 1) squared to the critic's loss. The next slides unpack each piece: where the checkpoints are, what the slope is, why minus 1, why squared, and what lambda does.

Part A · §24 · What is x̂?

x̂ sits between real and fake

x̂ = ε·x_real + (1 − ε)·x_fake, ε ~ Uniform(0, 1)

Real · 0

Fake · 10

x̂ = 6

ε = 0.4 → 0.4·0 + 0.6·10 = 6

Mix a real sample and a fake sample with a random weight epsilon. In practice epsilon is drawn fresh for each sample in the batch. With epsilon 0.4, x-hat sits at 6, between the two. For images, this is a pixel-wise blend of a real image and a generated one.

Part A · §25 · Why measure the gradient there?

The ideal critic has slope 1 on those lines

The optimal critic’s gradient norm is exactly 1 along straight lines joining real and fake points.

Checking everywhere is impossible. These lines are where the generator must travel.

Gulrajani et al., 2017

The notes say this is the region where we want guidance. The paper gives the deeper reason: for the optimal critic, the gradient norm is 1 almost everywhere along lines connecting coupled real and fake points. So sampling x-hat on those lines checks the condition exactly where it matters. It is a soft, sampled constraint, not a guarantee everywhere.

Part A · §26 · Gradient norm example

The slope is just an arrow’s length

∇C = [0.6, 0.8]

Across 0.6, up 0.8: the arrow is the hypotenuse.

‖∇C‖ = √(0.6² + 0.8²) = 1

Just right. In 1D the norm is simply |slope|: dC/dx = 4 → norm 4, too steep.

The gradient of the critic is an arrow: it points in the direction where the score rises fastest, and its length says how fast. The norm is that length, Pythagoras in 2D: across 0.6, up 0.8, length 1. For an image the arrow has one component per pixel, thousands of them, but the length is still the square root of the sum of squares. In one dimension the arrow has one component and its length is just the absolute slope.

Part A · §28 · Why square?

The penalty is a bowl centred at 1

━━ 10 · (norm − 1)² · x-axis: gradient norm, 0 → 3

norm 0 → 10 · norm 1 → 0 · norm 2 → 10 · norm 3 → 40

The penalty as a picture. Zero only at norm exactly 1, the bottom of the bowl. It grows on both sides, so a critic that is too flat is pushed to become steeper and one that is too steep is pushed to become flatter. Because the bowl is squared, being far off costs a lot: norm 3 costs 40, four times norm 2. Lambda = 10 sets how steep the walls are.

Part A · §29 · What is λ?

λ sets how hard we enforce it

A common value

λ = 10

Gradient norm = 1.3

(1.3 − 1)² = 0.09

GP = 10 × 0.09 = 0.9

λ controls the penalty’s strength. With λ = 10 and gradient norm 1.3, the penalty is 0.9: the number used in the full example two slides on.

Part A · §30 · Full WGAN-GP critic loss

One loss, two jobs

L_C = E[C(fake)] − E[C(real)] + λ·E[(‖∇C(x̂)‖₂ − 1)²]

Job 1

Score real high, fake low

E[C(fake)] − E[C(real)]

Example: 1.5 − 4.5 = −3

Job 2

Keep the slope near 1 between real and fake

λ·E[(‖∇C(x̂)‖ − 1)²]

Example: 10 × 0.3² = 0.9

The orange part is the plain WGAN critic loss from chapter 9. The blue part is the gradient penalty averaged over a batch of x-hat points. That is the complete WGAN-GP critic objective.

Part A · §33 · Critic learns the landscape first

Why train the critic 5× more?

The logic

  1. G follows the critic’s slope, like a map.
  2. A half-trained critic is a wrong map.
  3. W stays smooth, so a better critic only gives better directions.
stronger judgeeffect on G
classic Dworse: goes flat
WGAN criticbetter: closer to W

Key idea

In WGAN the judge can’t get “too good”, so we let it learn first.

Part A · §35 · Why .detach()?

.detach() only when training the critic

Critic step

fake = G(z).detach()

Use G’s output as plain data. No gradients flow into G.

Generator step

fake = G(z)

Gradients must flow back through C into G.

Detach during the critic step so the critic loss does not update or waste compute on the generator. Never detach during the generator step, or the generator receives no gradient at all. The generator step also leaves gradients on the critic's weights; that is harmless because opt_C.zero_grad() clears them before the next critic update.

Part A · §36 · BatchNorm note

Be careful with BatchNorm in a WGAN-GP critic

Why

The gradient penalty measures gradients per input. BatchNorm mixes information across the batch.

Common alternatives

No normalisation in the critic, LayerNorm, or other per-sample choices.

BatchNorm makes each sample’s score depend on the other samples in the batch, which interferes with per-sample gradients. lab-09 in your repo uses InstanceNorm, another per-sample choice.
