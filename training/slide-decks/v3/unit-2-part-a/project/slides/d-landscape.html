Part A · §5 · Strong gradient ≠ useful gradient

Near-perfect D: its score goes flat

D is steep only in the gap between real and fake, where no samples are.

Near-perfect D: flat floor under the fakes. A strong push, almost no direction.

Two discriminators' raw scores over a one-dimensional image space, with real data near x = 2 and fakes near x = 8. The solid curve is a near-perfect D, a(x) = 10·tanh(2(5 − x)): a high plateau over the real data and a flat floor under the fakes, where the slope is about 0.0005. In the gap at x = 5 the slope is 20, but no real or fake sample lives there, so it helps nobody. Compare the unsure D on the previous slide, a(x) = 2.75·tanh(0.25(5 − x)), whose slope at the fake is 0.41. The direction G receives is this slope at its own samples. As D approaches the perfect D*, exactly flat on each side, the slope goes to zero. Arjovsky and Bottou (2017): the original loss's gradient vanishes, and the −log D gradient becomes unstable.
