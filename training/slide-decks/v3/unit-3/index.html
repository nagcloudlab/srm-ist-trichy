Chapter 2: Why judging a GAN is hard.

Unit 3 · §2 · No answer key

A classifier has an answer key. A GAN doesn’t.

Classifier

Show it 10,000 labelled photos. Count how many it gets right: 94%. Done.

GAN

It draws a new face. Is it “right”? There is nothing to compare it with: no answer key.

Analogy

Grading a maths test vs judging a cooking contest: one has answers, the other needs a judge.

A classifier is easy to grade: there is a correct label for every test image. A generated face has no correct answer; it is new. So we need a different way to judge: like a cooking contest, where there is no answer sheet, only judges with good criteria.

Unit 3 · §2 · Quality and variety

A good GAN needs two things at once

Sharp but all the same

quality ✓ · variety ✗ (mode collapse)

Varied but blurry

quality ✗ · variety ✓

Blurry and the same

quality ✗ · variety ✗

Sharp and varied

quality ✓ · variety ✓ — the goal

The two words of the whole unit. Quality, also called fidelity: does each image look real? Variety, also called diversity: does the GAN produce the full range of the data? A score that checks only one of them can be fooled: one perfect face repeated forever has great quality and no variety.

Unit 3 · §2 · Tempting but wrong

Three tempting ways to judge, and why they fail

“Just look at it”

Subjective, can’t compare 50 models, easy to cherry-pick, and 16 samples can’t reveal missing variety.

“Use D’s loss”

D’s loss sits near the same value for good and bad GANs. A low loss can just mean a weak D.

“Compare pixels”

A good fake should not copy a real image; and shifting a face by one pixel changes every pixel.

So

We need a judge that understands images, and compares crowds, not single pictures.

The three shortcuts people still try. Looking at a grid is subjective and hides mode collapse. The discriminator’s loss does not track image quality: in the classic GAN it hovers near the same balance value whether the images are good or bad. Pixel distance punishes harmless shifts and rewards copying, which is memorisation, not generation.

Chapter 3: The first score: Inception Score.

Unit 3 · §3 · Inception Score

Ask an expert classifier to judge our images

generated images

→

Inception v3 · trained on ImageNet

→

what it sees in each image

Rewards quality

Each image gets a confident answer: “this is clearly a dog”.

Rewards variety

Across all images, the answers are spread over many classes.

Higher is better

Confident about each image, varied across images: a high Inception Score.

The Inception Score, 2016, borrows an expert: Inception v3, a classifier trained on ImageNet’s 1,000 classes. It looks at each generated image. If it is confident what each image shows, quality is good. If its answers cover many classes, variety is good. Higher is better. No formula needed: confident per image, varied overall.

Unit 3 · §3 · Its problems

Why the Inception Score fell out of favour

Never looks at real data

Perfect dogs score well even if we trained on cats.

Only knows ImageNet classes

Digits and faces aren’t its categories, so scores mean little.

Misses variety inside a class

Ten classes, but one image each? It can’t tell.

Fragile

Sample count and resizing change the score.

Analogy

A food critic who tastes only our dishes and never the recipe we were copying.

Four problems. The biggest: it never compares with the real data, so it cannot tell whether we generated the right kind of image. It only understands ImageNet’s classes. It misses collapse within a class. And it changes with implementation details. It is mostly historical now; you will meet it in older papers.

Chapter 4: FID: the standard score.

Unit 3 · §4 · Features, not pixels

Describe each image the way an expert would

an image

→

Inception v3, last layer before the answer

no classifying: just describing

→

round shape ✓

two eyes ✓

smiling ✓

… 2,048 such descriptions

Why

Two photos of the same face shifted by one pixel get almost the same description: features ignore harmless pixel changes.

FID starts like the Inception Score but stops one step earlier. Instead of asking Inception for a class, we take its 2,048 internal descriptions of the image: its features. Think of a fingerprint of what the image contains. Features capture meaning, so a one-pixel shift barely changes them, unlike raw pixels.

Unit 3 · §4 · Two crowds

FID compares two crowds of features

● real images  ·  ● generated images  ·  ● = centre of each crowd

Far apart

centres far apart: high FID

Same place, too narrow

variety missing: still a high FID

Same place, same spread

crowds match: low FID

FID

How far apart the two crowds are, in position and in spread. Lower is better; 0 means identical.

The heart of FID, no formula needed. Take thousands of real images and thousands of generated ones, turn each into features, and you get two crowds of points. FID asks two questions: are the crowds in the same place, and are they spread out the same way? Far apart: bad quality. Same place but too narrow: missing variety, mode collapse. Same place, same spread: low FID. So one number catches both quality and variety, and it compares with the real data.

A rough reading guide from the L12 lesson. Lower is better, the opposite of the Inception Score. The bands depend on the dataset and sample count, so use FID to compare models measured the same way, not to compare across papers blindly.

Chapter 5: FID in practice.

Unit 3 · §5 · The pipeline

FID in five steps

1 · Collect

Thousands of real images, and the same number of generated ones.

2 · Prepare

Resize every image the same way (299 × 299 for Inception).

3 · Describe

Run each through Inception v3 and keep its 2,048 features.

4 · Summarise

Find each crowd’s centre and spread.

5 · Compare

How far apart are the crowds? That is the FID.

The recipe, in words. In lab-13 students build each step from scratch; in practice the pytorch-fid package does it in one command. The important rule hides in step 2: real and fake images must be prepared identically.

Unit 3 · §5 · Tracking training

Use FID to decide when to stop

Read it

Falling: learning. Flat: converged. Rising: something broke. Keep the best checkpoint: epoch 25.

An illustrative run from the L13 lesson. FID falls fast, flattens around epoch 25, then rises again, which signals collapse or overfitting. So we save checkpoints and keep the one with the lowest FID, not the last one. This is far more reliable than watching the losses.

Unit 3 · §5 · Sample count

Too few images make FID look worse than it is

Rule

Two crowds from the same data should score 0. With few images they don’t. Use 10,000+ (50,000 is standard), and the same count for every model.

A simulation from the L13 lesson: two samples from the same distribution, so the true FID is zero. With 100 images the estimate is 21; it shrinks only as we add images. Small samples make FID too high, not just noisy. Always report the number of images, and use the same number when comparing models.

Unit 3 · §5 · Traps

Four ways FID can fool you

Different preparation

Resize real and fake images differently and FID changes by several points.

FID = 0 is suspicious

It may mean G copied the training images: memorisation, not creativity.

It can’t say why

FID says “worse”, not “blurry eyes” or “missing digits”.

Inception’s view of the world

Trained on ImageNet photos: less meaningful for X-rays or digits.

Four traps. Mismatched preprocessing silently changes the score. A perfect score can mean memorisation: check generated images against their nearest training images. FID reports how much, not what. And its judge, Inception, was trained on everyday photos, so it is a weaker judge for very different images. Lab-13’s FIDs use 2,000 images and torchvision weights, so they compare models within the lab, not with published papers.

Chapter 6: Beyond FID.

Unit 3 · §6 · Precision and recall

Precision = on target. Recall = covers the target.

Blue rings = the real data. ● = generated images.

High precision, low recall

every image looks real, but all from one spot: mode collapse

Low precision, high recall

covers everything, but many look fake: blurry

High precision, high recall

real-looking and covers the whole range: the goal

Precision and recall split FID’s single number back into its two parts. Precision: of our generated images, how many land inside the real data, i.e. look real? Recall: how much of the real data do our images cover? A collapsed GAN has high precision and low recall. A blurry GAN has the reverse. The goal is both.

A qualitative comparison, no maths. GANs give sharp images in a single fast pass, which is why they are still used for real-time and image-to-image work. Unit 3 · §7 · GAN disadvantages

Unstable training

Two networks in a tug of war; Unit 2’s fixes help but don’t cure it.

Mode collapse

Can skip parts of the data: sets up today’s bias problem.

No built-in score

Nothing tells us how well it fits the data: we need FID.

Copies its data’s flaws

Whatever is skewed in the data comes out skewed, often more so.

Strength

Still the fastest route to sharp images: one pass, real time.

Four weaknesses: unstable training, mode collapse, no built-in measure of fit, and inheriting the data’s flaws. The last two lead straight into the next chapters. Keep the strength in view too: speed and sharpness.

Chapter 8: Bias: who gets left out?.

Unit 3 · §8 · Three levels

Bias can enter at three points

1 · The data

The training set is unbalanced: e.g. mostly young, light-skinned faces.

2 · The model

The GAN amplifies the imbalance, and collapses on rare groups.

3 · The evaluation

FID averages over everyone, so a poorly served small group is hidden.

Definition

Bias = the outputs don’t fairly represent the variety of the real world, or even of the training data.

Three entry points. The data can be skewed. The model can make the skew worse. And the evaluation can hide it, because FID is an average over all images. The rest of this chapter takes them in turn.

Unit 3 · §8 · Data bias

The GAN learns what it is shown

CelebA, the best-known face dataset

About 77% of faces labelled “young”; mostly light-skinned, posed celebrity photos.

What a GAN learns from it

Older and darker-skinned faces are rarer, and come out blurrier.

CelebA, the most-used face dataset, is about 77% labelled young (and about 58% labelled female), mostly light-skinned, and made of posed celebrity photos. A GAN trained on it produces a “default face” to match, and the rarer faces get less training signal, so they are lower quality.

Unit 3 · §8 · Amplification

GANs don’t just copy the imbalance: they amplify it

Training data

→

GAN output

Why

More examples → more feedback → better at A → focuses even more on A. A vicious circle.

An illustrative example from L14: data split 70/30, output drifting to 85/15. The reason is feedback: the generator gets more learning signal from the bigger group, gets better at it, and leans further toward it. Research on face GANs has reported this amplification (Jain et al., 2020).

Unit 3 · §8 · Mode collapse as bias

Mode collapse doesn’t delete at random

common

survives

common

survives

rarer

dropped first

rarer

dropped first

Fairness view

When a GAN collapses to a few types, the majority survives and minorities disappear first.

Mode collapse becomes a fairness problem. If a face GAN collapses to five types instead of fifty, the survivors are the common ones; under-represented groups vanish first. On MNIST the same thing happens with digits: easy, common shapes survive while hard ones like 8 and 4 disappear. It is structural, not random.

Unit 3 · §8 · Real-world harm

Why it matters outside the lab

Synthetic training data

Fake faces train a face detector, which then works worse on under-represented faces.

Medical augmentation

Synthetic X-rays from one hospital’s patients make models worse for others.

Creative tools

Art tools trained mostly on one culture stereotype the rest.

Deepfake detection

Detectors trained on biased fakes miss fakes of other groups.

Four harms from L14. The common thread: GAN output often becomes another model’s training data, so bias travels downstream and turns into real discrimination.

Chapter 9: Detecting and reducing bias.

Unit 3 · §9 · Detect

Three steps to detect bias

1 · Audit the data

Count examples per group: digit, age, skin tone, hospital…

2 · Score each group

Generate per group and compute FID for each one separately.

3 · Look per group

Inspect samples for each group: are some blurrier or less varied?

The detection recipe. Count the groups in the data. Score each group separately: one overall FID can look fine while one group is poor. Then look at the samples per group. With a conditional GAN from Unit 2’s lab-10 you can generate each digit on demand and compute a per-digit FID with lab-13’s functions.

Unit 3 · §9 · Per-group FID

One overall score can hide a poorly served group

Read it

Overall 22 looks “good”, but group C at 61 is served badly. Average scores hide this.

Illustrative numbers, to show the idea. The overall FID is a respectable 22, but splitting by group reveals group C at 61. That is evaluation bias in action: the average is dominated by the big groups. Fairness checks always break the score down by group.

Unit 3 · §9 · Reduce

Five ways to reduce bias

Balance the data

Collect more of, or resample, the rare groups.

Condition on the group

A cGAN can be asked for each group equally (Unit 2).

Weight the loss

Count mistakes on rare groups more.

Evaluate per group

Report FID per group, not just overall.

Collect diverse data

The lasting fix: better data at the source.

Five strategies from L14. The first three change training; the last two change how we measure and what we collect. In practice teams combine several.

Unit 3 · §9 · The trade-off

A fairer GAN can have a worse FID, and that’s fine

The data is 80 / 20

FID rewards copying that split exactly, bias included.

We rebalance to 50 / 50

The output no longer matches the skewed data, so FID goes up.

Which is better?

The fairer model. A higher FID here is a feature, not a bug.

Lesson

Metrics measure match to the data, not fairness. Decide what “good” means for your use.

An honest tension. FID measures how well the output matches the training data, including its skew. Rebalancing for fairness moves away from that skew, so FID rises. That is not failure. A score is a tool; deciding what counts as good depends on how the GAN will be used and who could be harmed.

Chapter 10: StyleGAN: the state of the art.

Unit 3 · §10 · The problem it solves

In a normal GAN, one knob turns everything

before

→ turn one number in z →

after

pose, shape, smile, skin and hair all changed

StyleGAN’s goal

Separate controls for big things (pose) and small things (colour, freckles).

The Unit 2 Part B problem, entanglement, at full size. In DCGAN, the noise enters once at the start and every number affects everything. StyleGAN, NVIDIA 2019, redesigned the generator so different kinds of detail have different controls.

Unit 3 · §10 · The big idea

Paint like an artist: coarse to fine

1 · rough sketch

pose, face shape

→

2 · features

eyes, nose, mouth

→

3 · fine brush

colour, hair strands

StyleGAN

Give the artist a separate instruction at every stage, not one note at the start.

The analogy for StyleGAN. An artist sketches, then adds features, then fine colour and texture. StyleGAN builds images the same way, from a tiny 4×4 picture up to 1024×1024, and hands each stage its own style instruction.

Unit 3 · §10 · Four new parts

Four new parts, in plain words

Mapping network

A translator that turns random noise into a tidy “style code”: things that should be separate stay separate.

Learned blank canvas

Every image starts from the same learned canvas, not from noise.

A style dial at every layer

At each stage the style code resets the look: “wash, then paint”.

Sprinkled noise

Fresh random noise at each stage adds freckles and stray hairs.

The four ideas without any formulas. The mapping network translates noise into a style code where features are less tangled. The image always starts from one learned canvas. At every stage a style dial, called AdaIN, washes away the old look and paints the new one from the style code. And fresh noise adds the random fine detail real photos have. The full StyleGAN deck covers these with numbers.

Unit 3 · §10 · Style mixing

Mix two faces: A’s shape, B’s colouring

Person A

pose, shape, glasses

+

Person B

skin and hair colour

→

Mixed

A for the early stages, B for the late ones

Proof

Early stages control big things; late stages control small things. Each can take a different style.

Style mixing shows the idea works. Give person A’s style code to the early, coarse stages and person B’s to the late, fine stages: you get A’s pose, face shape and glasses with B’s colouring. Impossible in a DCGAN.

Unit 3 · §10 · Two more dials

Noise for detail, truncation for safety

Same style, new noise

Only the stray hairs move. The person stays the same.

The truncation dial

Pull every face toward the average face: fewer odd results, but less variety.

Link to FID

Truncation is the precision vs recall trade-off as a dial: more quality, less variety.

Two more controls. Change only the noise and just the fine random details change. The truncation trick pulls every style toward the average face: images get safer and cleaner, but more alike. That is exactly precision versus recall from chapter 6, as a dial.

Unit 3 · §10 · Why it matters

Why StyleGAN set the standard

Photorealistic faces

The first GAN whose 1024 × 1024 faces fooled most people.

Separate controls

Pose, features and texture can be edited independently.

Cleaner directions

Part B’s latent directions work far better in its style space.

Kept improving

StyleGAN2 removed blob artifacts; StyleGAN3 stopped textures sticking to pixels.

Still has the bias problem

Trained on skewed faces, it reproduces the skew: audit it too.

Why StyleGAN matters. Realistic faces, independent controls, and a style space where editing directions work cleanly. Later versions fixed visual artifacts. And the last row ties the unit together: even the best GAN inherits its data’s bias, so every chapter today applies to it.
